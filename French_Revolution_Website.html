01 / Presenter 1 · Background and causes

Why did a revolution begin?

An unequal society

The First Estate was the clergy; the Second Estate was the nobility. The diverse Third Estate included peasants, workers, merchants and professionals.

Inherited privileges and unequal burdens created resentment.

A financial crisis

War debts strained royal finances. Attempts to reform taxation faced resistance.

Poor harvests in 1788 and rising bread prices increased hardship for ordinary families.

New political ideas

Enlightenment thinkers questioned established authority.

Voltaire discussed tolerance, Rousseau popular sovereignty, and Montesquieu the separation of powers.

Social inequality, economic hardship and political conflict reinforced one another. Louis XVI called the Estates-General in May 1789.

02 / Presenter 2 · Political change

From monarchy to republic.

1791–1792

A constitution limited the king's powers in 1791. His attempted escape damaged trust.

War and conflict intensified. The monarchy was abolished in September 1792, and France became a republic.

1793–1794

Louis XVI was executed on 21 January 1793.

During the Terror, revolutionary authorities imprisoned and executed real or suspected opponents. Robespierre was an important figure, but not the only person responsible.

1795–1799

The Directory governed from 1795 amid continuing instability.

Napoleon's coup in November 1799 established the Consulate, commonly marking the Revolution's end.

03 / Presenter 3 · Legacy

Lasting ideals. Unfinished promises.

Liberty

Freedom within a framework that respects the rights of others.

Equality

Equal treatment before the law and a challenge to inherited privilege.

Fraternity

Solidarity and responsibility towards others.

The Revolution promoted citizenship and national sovereignty. These ideas influenced later debates about constitutions and government. The three-word motto has revolutionary roots but became established later.

Rights were not equal for everyone. Women did not gain voting rights. Olympe de Gouges demanded political equality in her 1791 Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen.

France's 14 July commemoration recalls the Bastille in 1789 and the Festival of the Federation in 1790.

03 / Presenter 3 · Conclusion

A revolution that still matters.

The French Revolution transformed institutions and introduced powerful ideals of citizenship, liberty and equality.

War, repression and exclusion also revealed the gap between declaring rights and protecting them in practice.

Discussion: How can societies pursue justice while protecting human life and individual freedom?
